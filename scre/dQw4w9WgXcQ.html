Gen Z Brawl Stars Player Can Not Camp in Bush Because of Their Restlessness
Coaches and analysts say campers built for three-minute stakeouts are being replaced by a generation that struggles to sit still past three seconds — and it's costing them matches.
By A. Correspondent · Esports & Behavioral Desk
Sept. 16, 2026 ⤴ Share 💬 128 ☆ Save

A competitor breaks cover well before the optimal camping window closes. Analysts say the average bush stay has shortened every season since ranked play began.

Last week, across dozens of ranked matches, a pattern that coaches had only joked about started showing up in the data: players believed to be Gen Z were repeatedly failing to hold a bush-camping position long enough for it to pay off. Camping, the tactic of sitting motionless in foliage to ambush an approaching opponent, is generally understood to require up to three full minutes of patience to reach peak effectiveness. Many younger players, observers say, are breaking cover in well under half that time.

"The read into the bush was perfect," said one spectator who watched a recent match unravel. "And then ten seconds later they just walked out and died for no reason." Analysts have taken to calling the phenomenon a "collapse of tactical patience," and several point to years of exposure to short-form video — TikTok clips, Shorts, fifteen-second loops — as a plausible contributing factor.

"They could have won by waiting three minutes. Instead they waited three seconds." — An anonymous ranked coach

Psychologists interviewed for this story were careful not to frame the behavior as a diagnosis, but they note that shortening attention spans are frequently discussed as a broader generational pattern. In match footage, many of the players in question didn't even fill the waiting period with secondary behavior — checking teammates' chat, glancing at the minimap — that older players use to pass the time in cover. Instead, they simply tapped the screen until the urge to move won out.

"Players used to be able to sit in a bush for five minutes without a word," said one coach, speaking on condition of anonymity. "Now they can't sit through a loading screen." Younger players see it differently. "Standing still in a bush just isn't it," one ranked player countered. "If I'm not moving, I feel more anxious, not less." The disagreement, some researchers argue, reflects a deeper generational shift in what "waiting" even means.
